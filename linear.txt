SEC Approach and assumptions

----------------------------------------------------------------------

What is a linear model?

	A \emph{fit} to a \textbf{response variable} based on assumptions about its relationship to one or more \textbf{predictor variables}

	\emph{Inferences} about parameters based on assumptions about the \emph{conditional} distribution of the response variable

		Assume the response variable has “true” distribution for any value of the predictor variables

			PREANS An example

			STUDENTS Use temperature to predict deaths	

	A linear model gives the response variable as a linear combination of the \emph{parameters} it is estimating

		OLDANS It is not necessarily linear with respect to predictor variables!

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Linear model?

$y$ is response, $x$ is predictor

	$y = a + b x$

	$y = a + b x + c x^2 + d x^3$

	$y = \frac{a}{b+x}$

	$y = a x^b$

	$\log(y) = \log a + b \log(x)$

	OLDANS 1, 2 and 5 are lms

		OLDANS Linear in a, b, ...

	OLDANS 4 and 5 are the same equation, but imply a different \emph{distribution} for the response variable $y$

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Linear model?

	What if $x$ is a categorical variable?

		OLDANS \emph{Any} response to a categorical variable can be encoded as a linear model!

	CONV What do we mean by linear in the parameters?

		CONV Consists of “terms” that are added together

		CONV Each term is multiplied by a single parameter, one time

Assumptions

	Linear-model \emph{fits} are basically always correct, but can be hard to interpret

		You can find the “best” parameters

		But you need assumptions to know what that means

	Linear-model \emph{inferences} (parameter estimates P-values, CIs) depend on assumptions about \emph{residuals}

	A residual is $y-\hat y$: the difference between an observed value $y$ and a model prediction $\hat y$

	lm inference assumes that residuals are independent and normally distributed, with constant variance

		The mean of the residuals is always 0

		OLDANS Otherwise the machine would find a better linear model

The Central Limit Theorem

	Most distributions converge to a normal distribution if you add up independent random variables and take the mean

		Variables that result from additive processes will tend to be normal

		COMMENT Variables that result from multiplicative processes will tend to be lognormal

		Tests based on normality assumptions will be less sensitive to that assumption when there is more data

			There is adding and averaging going on inside the test

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Scientific question

	Height can mediate the relationship between age and lung capacity

		ANS People might be taller because they're older

		ANS We might want to exclude it to see direct effects of age

		ANS Or include it to see total effects

		PENDING exclude/include are backwards!!

	Age cannot be caused by height

		ANS We should (usually) include it to see the effect of height

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Controlling

	Adding a second variable when our primary interest is the first is called “controlling”

		ANS We control for age when we study the effects of smoking on lung capacity

		ANS Also the effect of sex, height, ..., maybe

		PENDING Relevant medical conditions if available

	When you control for a variable, you don't necessarily need to interpret the parameters of that variable at all!

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Conflation

	Why do children who smoke have higher lung capacity?

		ANS Because they're older

		ANS Age causes both of these things, separately from any effect they have on each other

	This is called conflation, or confounding

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Accounting for variation

	Even when we don't have conflation, controlling for relevant predictors can make our predictions more accurate

	Example, randomized controlled trial of a blood-pressure drug

		ANS No conflation -- the only thing that effects our main predictor is the coin!

		ANS Still want to control for things

----------------------------------------------------------------------

How many parameters?

	Statistics isn't magic

		The linear model will find patterns even in noise

	Controlling for too many factors will make our predictions less \emph{precise}

		ANS Add variation, uncertainty

	Rule of thumb:

		5--10 solid, continuous data points per parameter

		When data points are small counts, or yes/no, we need to reduce parameters further

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Which parameters?

	It's very common to choose predictors based on preliminary analyses

	Or to throw out interaction or curvature terms based on significance

	This is accepting the null hypothesis (concluding these things don't matter)

	It's also introducing confirmation bias

		If we choose our final test based on the data, we will be bad frequentists

		ANS wrong more than 5% of the time

CONT Which parameters?

	Decide how many parameters you can afford based on the data

	Decide which parameters to use based on scientific judgment

		Can be based on other data, but not based on data from your study

SS Interactions

----------------------------------------------------------------------

What is an interaction?

	So far, each predictor has its own effect, and the effects add up

		The effect of age on lung capacity is assumed to be the same for boys and girls

	An \textbf{interaction} means that the effect of one predictor depends on the value of another

		Does lung capacity increase faster with age for boys than for girls?

	Interactions are symmetric

		ANS If the effect of age depends on sex, then the effect of sex depends on age

		ANS We can choose whichever way of saying it matches our question

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Interactions in R

	lm($y \sim x + z$)

		Additive model: no interaction

	lm($y \sim x * z$)

		Short for lm($y \sim x + z + x:z$)

		The new “lm variable” is made by multiplying $x$ and $z$

	Is this still a linear model?

		ANS Yes! It's linear in the \emph{parameters}

	How many parameters does an interaction add?

		ANS Multiply the parameters of the two variables

		ANS Continuous and two categories: 1

		ANS Three categories and four categories: $2 \times 3 = 6$

	Example classPenguins (based on lmAsn)

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Interpreting parameters

	$y = a + b x + c z + d xz$, where $z$ is 0 for baseline, and 1 for the other group

		Baseline group: intercept is $a$, slope is $b$

		ANS Other group: intercept is $a+c$, slope is $b+d$

	What does $d$ mean?

		ANS The \emph{difference} between the slopes

	What does $c$ mean now?

		ANS The difference between the groups when $x$ is zero

		ANS This may not be interesting, or even make sense (height zero?)

		ANS Centering $x$ can make it mean something

	Parameters for the other variables are no longer overall effects

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Main effects

	Your model is now estimating many different possible effects for one predictor

		ANS effect is different when other predictors change

	Depending on your scientific question, you can:

		Ignore these

		Average over them (may return to that)

	This confusion is often used as an excuse to throw out non-significant interactions

		ANS Do not do that!

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Is one group changing more than another?

	Treatment group changes significantly; control group does not

		Can we say that treatment group changed more?

		ANS No! Significant and not significant are not (necessarily) significantly different

		ANS Failing to reject for the control group doesn't mean it didn't change

	The interaction tests the question directly

		ANS Group $\times$ time: how different are the changes?

		ANS The estimate and CI are for the difference we actually care about

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Interactions and scale

	If effects combine by multiplying, they look like an interaction on the linear scale

		ANS A treatment that increases growth by 10% adds more grams to big animals

		ANS On the log scale, there may be no interaction

	Whether there's an interaction can depend on the scale you choose

		ANS Decide what scale makes scientific sense before interpreting

		ANS This is why transformations and interactions both came up under linearity

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Which interactions?

	A non-significant interaction doesn't mean that there is no interaction

		ANS Throwing it out is accepting the null hypothesis

	Same answer as for other parameters:

		ANS Decide how many you can afford, and choose based on scientific judgment

	It's often good to boil things down to a one-parameter interaction

	Beyond that, things can get complicated.

Example: comparing to the control group

	We test reflex times in two groups of rabbits

		One exposed to radiation, and one not

			ANS usually the non-radiation group is a sham, where you pretend to expose them to radiation

	Naive method: show that the sham group doesn't change, and the radiation group does

		ANS Better method: show that the sham group changes \emph{differently} (hypothesis is that they will be \emph{relatively} slower)

	Typically done with interactions

		Although sometimes it could be done more simply with differences
