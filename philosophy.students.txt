UNIT 3: Statistical philosophy

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Overview

	We use statistics to confirm effects, estimate parameters, and predict outcomes

	I think smoking is bad for lung capacity in children

		\emph{Confirmation:} Children who smoke have lower exhalation volumes (ev)

		\emph{Estimation:} The average non-smoker has an ev 1.1--1.3 times higher than a smoker

		\emph{Prediction:} A 14-year-old male smoker will smoker will have an ev of 2.6--3.0 L/s

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Some definitions

	a \textbf{variable} is something that we measure

		flower species, rodent weight, provincial area

	a \textbf{statistic} is something that we calculate from our
	variables

		mean weight, median area, correlation between weight and leg length

	a \textbf{parameter} is something we estimate using a statistical model

		the difference between soil wetness surrounding two different types of plants

		parameters are estimated with uncertainty

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example: Vitamin A

	We compare health indicators of children treated or not treated with vitamin A supplements

		\emph{Estimate:} how much healthier (or less healty) are the treated children on average?

		\emph{Confirmation:} are we sure that the supplements are helping (or hurting)?

		\emph{Range of estimates:} how much do we think the supplement is helping?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

P values and confidence intervals

	We use \emph{P values} to say how sure we are that we have seen a positive effect

	We use \emph{confidence intervals} to say what we think is going on (with a certain level of confidence)

	P values are \emph{over-rated}

	\emph{Never} use a high P value as (direct) evidence for anything, e.g.:

		that an effect is small

		that two quantities are similar

	What does a P value actually measure?

TSEC Frequentist paradigm

	Choose an allowable mistake rate, $\alpha$

		COMMENT Because Fisher made that up as an example!

	The paradigm is: our probability of being wrong should be $\leq\alpha$

	If we do a lot of studies (correctly), we won't wrong more than $\alpha$ of the time

	An approach that correctly limits the probability of being wrong is \textbf{valid}

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Frequentist P-value logic

	I define a P value as the probability under the null of getting a value at least as extreme as my observation

	Hypothetically (or counter-factually) if the null is true, what will my P value be?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Comparing different tails

	Let's say I have 5 outcomes and my null hypothesis is that they are all equally likely.

		I observe 10% (instead of 20%) of the key outcome

		Is that more or less “extreme” than observing 35%?

	I start with 10g of calcium in a mesocosm and expect that to say the same?

		What lower value is as extreme as 20g?

Confidence interval logic

	To reject the null hypothesis, we ask:

		assuming the null is true, what is the probability under the null of getting a value at least as extreme as my observation?

	To reject a value from our confidence interval, we ask:

		assuming the value is true, what is the probability under the null of getting a value at least as extreme as my observation?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Confidence intervals vs.~P values

	CIs are more useful

	They also require more assumptions

		If effects are absent, you don't need any assumptions about them

		To assume that treated children grow better, we need assumptions about the relationship between distributions

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Paradigm summary

	We want to “fail to reject” the null hypothesis at least $1-\alpha$ of the time, under the assumption that it's true

	We (usually) do this because we want to test confidence in the \emph{sign} of an effect or difference

Statistical error types

	Type I (\emph{False positive:}) concluding there is an effect when
	there isn't one

		This doesn't happen in biology.  There is always an effect.

		But it is useful to think about as a counter-factual

	Type II (\emph{False negative:}) concluding there is no effect when
	there really is

		This \emph{should} never happen, because we should never conclude
		there is no effect

		We should think about “failure to find” and “power to find”

			Later in the term

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Doing Science

	Once you collect data:

	You should never make a false-negative error.

	Or a false-positive error

	\emph{Sign \chinese{($\pm$)} error:}
	if I think an effect is positive, when it's really
	negative (or vice versa)

	\emph{Magnitude \chinese{(大小)} error:} if I think an effect is small, when
	it's really large (or vice versa)

	Confidence intervals aid clear thinking

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Small effects

	We should't build statistical confidence that something is small by
	failing to see it clearly

	We must instead see clearly that it is small

	This means we need a standard (our cutoff) for what we mean by small
