UNIT 5: Statistical practice

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Overview

	We have learned a bit about particular statistical tests

	Real science: many possible tests, limited data, choices made along the way, and only some results get seen

	This unit: how to plan a study, how to read a result, and how to avoid fooling yourself

		Statistics still isn't magic

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Prediction vs.~inference

	Recall: “we use statistics to confirm, estimate and predict”

	\emph{Prediction:} will the model give good answers for new observations?

		Parameters only matter as a means to that end

	\emph{Inference:} what can we say about a particular effect, and how sure are we?

		Estimates and CIs need to be honest

	A lot of model-choice advice is built for prediction

		Dropping predictors that don't look important (significance, or “information criteria” like AIC)

		OLDANS Bad for frequentism, lead to over-confidence

	This course will focus on inference, not prediction

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example

	If we want to use a small data set to figure out how to estimate lung capacity without measuring it, we might drop age or height 

		OLDANS Prediction

	If we want to understand the effect of height, we can't drop age.

		OLDANS Inference

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSEC Study design

	The most important statistical decisions are made before there are any data

		Analysis can't fix a study that's not designed to answer the research question

	CONV How do I translate my scientific question into a statistical question?

		What quantity do I want to estimate, and for what population?

	How small an effect can I afford to miss?

		OLDANS How big an effect is “important”?

		OLDANS Based on scientific understanding, not the study data

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSS Experiments

	The gold standard for scientific inference

	Control

		Compare different treatments

	Replicate

		More than one independent unit in each treatment group

	Randomize

		breaks the link between treatment and everything else

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Controlled experiments

	A controlled experiment compares different treatment groups

		You don't need a “control” group (e.g., radioactive rabbits)

			OLDANS Chicken feed example, flu mask example

	Groups should be as similar as possible, except for the thing that we want to compare

		OLDANS We don't keep the two groups of rabbits separate; we only separate them for the trip to the radioactive room

		OLDANS If we are giving village children vitamin A, we give the control group a similar pill without vitamin A

		OLDANS We (usually) don't do one treatment in one neighborhood and another treatment in another neighborhood

			OLDANS If we do, we need a statistical model that doesn't treat children in the same neighborhood as independent

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Blinding or masking

	Human subjects should not know what group they're in

		STUDENTS Not always practical

	Ideally, researchers should know as little as possible about who is in whose group

		Especially important for researchers making measurements, especially qualitative measurements

		Also important for statisticians who need to make decisions during the study!

			OLDANS We can also try to make all of the statistical decisions before the study

			OLDANS And \emph{journal} any decisions we need to make during the study

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Replication

	Units of replication under the same treatment (\textbf{replicates}) are independent

		CONV Different units under the same treatment have nothing in common except the treatment itself

			Except what they share with everyonoe

		Must be randomized independently

		Avoid treating them together, or assessing them together

			OLDANS Don't assess all of your irradiated rabbits in the morning and the sham rabbits in the afternoon

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Pseudoreplication

	I have one radioactive rabbit and one non-radioactive rabbit

		CONV I measure each of them 12 times and do a linear model on the combined data from each treatment:

	This is a valid test of \blank\, but an invalid test of \blank.

		OLDANS the difference between these two rabbits; the effects of radioactivity

	OR I have two radioactive rabbits and two non-radioactive rabbits

		CONV I measure each rabbit 12 times and do a linear model comparing the 24 irradiated and 24 sham measurements

		OLDANS invalid

	Mixing levels like this is called \textbf{pseudo-replication}

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Randomization

	People are very bad at randomizing, and very good at unconscious bias

	Randomize with a coin or computer

	Make as many choices as you can \emph{before} randomizing

		OLDANS Divide your mice randomly into groups before deciding which group gets which treatment 

	Trying to pick randomly using your brain is called \emph{haphazard} sampling

		catching animals and tagging them for a particular treatment group

		OLDANS Don't do this

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Covariates

	In a randomized experiment, covariates don't make your experiment \emph{less valid}, but they can make it \emph{less powerful}

		OLDANS Validity: what is the probability that the CIs will include the true value?

		OLDANS Power: how clearly can I see? e.g., how small are the CIs?

		CONV Gold standard: in the counterfactual where nothing is happening, I should falsely see something clear $<\alpha$ of the time.

	Deal with covariates by blocking or statistical modeling

		OLDANS If you think they're important

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Blocking

	Structure the experiment to reduce effects of covariates

		OLDANS Randomize boys and girls separately

		OLDANS Randomize inside each school

		OLDANS Paired t-tests are an example of blocking (study different fingers from the same children!)

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Statistical modeling

	Adding covariates can help make your estimate more precise

		Older people are more likely to have heart disease

		Taller children are likely to gain more weight

	Remember: 5--10 data points per parameter

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSS Observational studies

	Why are controlled experiments (as opposed to statistical control) the gold standard?

		OLDANS Statistical control can never control for everything

	Why do we do observational studies anyway?

		OLDANS Practicality – controlled studies may be difficult or impossible

		OLDANS We can't randomize children to smoke or not

	In some fields, conclusions can only be based on experiments

		OLDANS Observational studies can still be useful for exploration

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example: HIV control

	Observational studies suggested that male circumcision reduced HIV transmission

		OLDANS People who are circumcised have different cultural contexts

		OLDANS Being circumcised as a child may affect behaviour

	Only later did people attempt large, randomized experiments

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example: exercise

	People who are more active have better health outcomes

	Is that because:

		OLDANS Exercise improves health?

		OLDANS People with fewer medical conditions exercise more and have better outcomes?

	OLDANS This is an example of confounding

	How might you test?

		OLDANS Institute education programs for half of the patients in a group

----------------------------------------------------------------------

What to control for

	More control is not always better; the answer depends on causal structure

	\emph{Confounder:} causes both predictor and response

		OLDANS Control for it (age, for smoking)

	\emph{Mediator:} lies on the path from predictor to response

		OLDANS Depends on the question (height, for age)

	\emph{Collider:} caused by both predictor and response

		OLDANS Don't control for it: this creates an association

	Draw the arrows before you look at the data

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Natural experiments

	A strong kind of observational study

	Two similar, nearby communities are in different states

	One state has high alcohol taxes, and the corresponding community drinks less alcohol

		OLDANS Only one replicate per treatment

		OLDANS Still potentially useful for exploration

	What if you have dozens of states with different alcohol taxes?

		OLDANS This is a replicated experiment

		OLDANS But you still can't control for everything

----------------------------------------------------------------------

More natural experiments

	Streams in areas that have been logged vs.~those that haven't.

		Or upstream and downstream of a development

	Villages where a health intervention has been implemented vs.~those where it is still scheduled for implementation

		OLDANS Better if this could actually be randomized!

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Correlation vs causation

	Correlation is what we observe directly

		OLDANS Children who smoke tend to have lower lung capacity

	Causation refers to mechanism

		OLDANS Smoking lowers the lung capacity of children

	Statistical evidence can directly support causation

		OLDANS Only in a randomized experiment

	Statistical evidence can indirectly support causation

		OLDANS When combined with scientific evidence

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSS Simulation

	We rarely know the truth about real data

	If we make up data, we know the truth, and we can see how our method does

	A few lines of R can replace a lot of formulas

	It also forces us to say what we think our study looks like

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Simulating a study

	Choose a truth: effect size, noise, sample size, study structure

	Generate a fake data set

	Analyze it exactly the way you plan to analyze the real data

	Save the estimate, CI and P value

	Repeat many times, and count

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Testing validity

	Set the effect to zero: does the method reject $\leq\alpha$ of the time?

		OLDANS This is the frequentist definition of validity, tested directly

	P values under the null should look uniform

	Set the effect to something else: do the CIs contain the truth $1-\alpha$ of the time?

		This is called \textbf{coverage}

	Example: fishTanks

		See upcoming virtual lecture

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Testing power

	Set the effect to the smallest value you care about

	Count how often you get a clear answer

	Change the sample size, and repeat

	Example: powerTest.R

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Limits of simulation

	You only learn about the world you simulated

		ANS Statistics isn't magic, and neither is fake data

	Break your assumptions on purpose: skewed noise, unequal variance, outliers, clusters

	How much does each one matter for the inference?

		Recall: normality matters least; independence matters most

	Fake data is also good for debugging an analysis before the real data arrive

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSEC Power and precision

	\textbf{Power:} the probability of seeing the sign of an effect clearly, if the effect has a given size

		Power belongs to a study \emph{and} a hypothetical effect

	Low power doesn't just waste resources; it also misleads

		Coming up: the significance filter

	A common target is 80%

		COMMENT Like $\alpha=0.05$, this is mostly a habit

----------------------------------------------------------------------

What determines power?

	Effect size, noise, sample size, $\alpha$

	Standard error goes down with $\sqrt{n}$

		ANS Twice the precision takes four times the data

	Design can be as important as sample size

		Better measurements, blocking, covariates

	Interactions usually need more data than main effects

----------------------------------------------------------------------

What effect size to use?

	Power analysis needs an effect size, and statistics can't choose it

	Best choice: the smallest effect that would matter

	Bad choice: the effect you need to justify the sample size you already have

	Questionable choice: a published or pilot estimate

		ANS These tend to be too big

	Good choice: scientific information at a different level

		ANS Would this much growth improve the health outcome?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Planning for precision

	Better question: how wide will my confidence interval be?

	Would that interval tell me something useful?

		Could it separate “small” from “large”?

		Example: flu masks and non-inferiority

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Power after the fact

	“Observed power” calculated from your own estimate is just your P value in disguise

		ANS It adds no information

	It can't rescue a high P value

	ANS After the study, the CI tells you what the study did and didn't see

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSS The significance filter

	We tend to pay attention to results only when they are “significant”

	To be significant, an estimate has to be big compared to its standard error

	If the study is noisy, only overestimates can get through the filter

	So the estimates we see are biased, even if each study was done correctly

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Sign and magnitude errors

	We can use simulation to investigate the results of under-powered studies

	Magnitude: how much does a significant estimate exaggerate the truth?

	Sign: how often does a significant estimate have the wrong sign?

	Example: lowPower.R

----------------------------------------------------------------------

The winner's curse

	The same thing happens whenever you pick the best-looking result

		The strongest of many effects, the top gene, the best predictor

	First reports tend to be the biggest

	Later estimates shrink, without anything having changed

		Regression to the mean

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Publication bias

	The same filter operates at the level of the literature

		Authors, reviewers and journals all prefer clear results

	The file drawer: unclear results disappear

	Replication projects tend to find smaller effects

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSEC Base rates and false discoveries

	A P value tells us about the data, given a hypothetical

	We want to know about the world, given the data

	These aren't the same thing, and you need more information to get from one to the other

	Key extra information: how plausible was this before we looked?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Screening example

	A disease affects 1% of people; the test catches 90% of cases, and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people

	You test positive. How worried should you be?

		ANS Of 1000 people: 10 sick, 9 flagged; 990 healthy, about 50 flagged

		ANS Only about 15% of positives are sick

	Most people guess much higher

	Counting people works better than using formulas

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Simple Bayesian logic

	What you should believe depends on what you believed, and on how strongly the evidence discriminates

	Posterior odds = prior odds $\times$ likelihood ratio

	Strong evidence can't always overcome a very low prior

	This is not the same thing as Bayesian statistics

		We can use Bayesian logic and keep our frequentist paradigm

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Science as a screening test

	Treat a study as a test applied to a hypothesis

	$\alpha$ is the false-alarm rate, power is the sensitivity, and the proportion of true hypotheses is the prevalence

	1000 hypotheses, 10% true, $\alpha=0.05$

		ANS Power 0.8: 80 true hits and 45 false alarms

		ANS Power 0.2: 20 true hits and 45 false alarms

	So: a “significant” result in a low-power study of an unlikely idea is weak evidence

----------------------------------------------------------------------

But the null is never true!

	The null is a \emph{counterfactual}: what would we see if there were nothing to see?

	It puts a ceiling on sign errors

		The probability of your observation if the truth is going the other direction …

		ANS Is less than if the truth is zero

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Plausibility in practice

	Surprising results are more likely to be wrong

		They are also more likely to be published and publicized

	Ask what is already known about the mechanism

	Prior knowledge is a legitimate part of scientific judgment, even if it isn't part of your P value

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSS Multiple comparisons

	If I test 20 things that have no effect at $\alpha=0.05$, the chance of at least one “finding” is 64%

	Every individual test was valid. What went wrong?

	At what level should we control $\alpha$?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Scale of error

	What counts as being wrong?

		ANS A single parameter, a variable, an experiment, a paper, a career?

	The answer depends on what you are going to do with the result

		And on what a mistake costs

	There is no single correct scale, so this is a scientific decision

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Controlling the chance of any error

	\textbf{Family-wise error rate:} the probability of \emph{any} false positive in a family of tests

	Bonferroni: use $\alpha/m$ for $m$ tests

		$\alpha$ spending, again

		Simple, valid, and conservative

	Variable-level tests: one test for a multi-parameter variable

		Then ask about particular contrasts

----------------------------------------------------------------------

False discovery rate

	\textbf{FDR:} what proportion of the things I am calling discoveries are false?

		Also based on a null counter-factual

		And a weird paradigm:

			my expected FDR over a number of hypothetical experiments is what is controlled

	Often used for screens with many things to test: genes, species

		You are choosing what to follow up, not drawing a final conclusion

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Fewer questions

	The best multiple-comparisons correction is to not need one

	Focus on a small set of questions, each with a small number of parameters

	Exploratory analysis is fine if it is called exploratory

		Results are ideas for a new study

	Corrections should be used sparingly

	Corrections don't fix the significance filter

		The survivors of a stricter filter are even more exaggerated

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSEC P-hacking and forking paths

	Corrections work if you know how many tests you did

	In practice, the real number of possible analyses is much larger, and often hidden

	This is the multiple-comparison problem without the bookkeeping

	PRESENT Example: Listening to children's music makes you younger

	NOTES HREF https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22006061/ 
	Example: Listening to children's music makes you younger

----------------------------------------------------------------------

P-hacking

	Trying analyses until something crosses the line

	The usual tools

		Extra outcomes, extra covariates, subgroups, outlier removal, transformations

	A few of these together can push false-positive rates to around 60%

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Forking paths

	You don't need to try many analyses, or to do anything dishonest

	You only need to have chosen your one analysis in a way that depends on the data

		ANS With different data, you would have made different reasonable choices

	PRESENT The Garden of Forking paths

	NOTES HREF https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/research/unpublished/p\_hacking.pdf
	The Garden of Forking paths

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Optional stopping

	“Let's collect a few more and check again”

	Each peek is another chance to cross the line

	Stopping rules can be valid, but they need to be planned

		Clinical trials do this with $\alpha$ spending

----------------------------------------------------------------------

TSS Model choice

	Choosing a model after seeing the data is one of the commonest forks

	Decide how many parameters you can afford, and choose them with scientific judgment

	A lot of standard advice points the wrong way

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Dropping terms

	Dropping “non-significant” terms

		ANS Accepts the null hypothesis

		ANS Doesn't always account for dropped “comparisons”

	What's left gets CIs that are too narrow and estimates that are too large

	AIC-based selection is not a way around this for inference

		ANS It's for prediction

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Collinearity

	Predictors in observational data are often correlated

		Age and height; temperature and elevation

	A model asks: what is the effect of one, holding the other fixed?

	If they rarely vary separately, the data say little about that

	Parameter CIs get wide

	That is the correct answer!

		ANS The model is telling you can't see clearly

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Variance inflation factors

	Ask: how much bigger is the variance of this parameter than it would be with uncorrelated predictors?

	Standard advice: if VIF is more than 5 (or 10), drop a predictor

	What does JD think of this advice?

		ANS It's a bad idea

----------------------------------------------------------------------

The trouble with VIF rules

	Dropping a confounder doesn't remove the problem, it hides it

		ANS CI gets narrower

		ANS Estimate may not mean what was originally intended

	It's another data-driven fork

----------------------------------------------------------------------

What to do instead

	Ask what question you actually want answered

	Report the wide CI honestly, and say what the data can't separate

	If you can't answer your question

		ANS Get more data

		ANS or a better study design

----------------------------------------------------------------------

SEC Good practice

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Analysis plans

	Write down your question, model and main comparisons before you look

		Simulated data lets you write the code in advance

	Preregistration: making this plan public and time-stamped

	A plan isn't a prison

		Deviations are fine, if they are reported

	This course: plan for the projects

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Journalling

	Keep a complete statistical journal:

		Everything you looked at, everything you decided

		Exclusions, variable definitions, scales, model terms, comparisons

	Which of these did you make after looking?

	Which ones could change your conclusion?

	Allows you and others to honestly evaluate your philosophical approach

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Confirmatory and exploratory

	Both are valuable; the problem is mixing them up

	Label each result in your write-up

	Report exploratory results as descriptions, and as ideas

	Use your findings to plan new experiments

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Sensitivity analysis

	Would a different reasonable choice change the conclusion?

	Report the alternatives, not just the favourite

		We talked about this with outliers

	Multiverse analysis: run all the reasonable forks at once

	If the answer depends on a fork, then that's part of the answer

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Reporting results

	Estimates and CIs first, with units

		Compare them to something real

	Report everything you did, including the unclear results

	Show the data

	Don't throw away information with thresholds: $P=0.049$ and $P=0.051$ are the same result

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Clarity language

	More practice with the language from the philosophy unit

	Rewrite sentences from real papers

		“There was no effect of ...”

		“X did not differ between groups, therefore ...”

		“Marginally significant”, “trending towards significance”

	Say what you saw clearly, and what you didn't

	Claim “small” only when you can clearly see that it is small

	ADD Dushoff, Kain and Bolker 2019

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Significant and not significant

	Still a very common error in the literature

		The difference between “significant” and “not significant” is not necessarily significant

	Example: flu deaths, and the rabbits

	To compare two effects, estimate the difference

	Exercise: find an example in a recent paper in your field

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Reproducible work

	Scripts, not clicks

	Keep the raw data unchanged

	Someone else (or you, next year) should be able to rerun everything

	This also gives you a record of your forks

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Reading a paper

	What was the unit, and how many were there?

	Was the analysis planned? How many forks and tests were there?

	What power did it have for an effect that matters?

	How plausible was this before the study?

	Is the estimate likely to be exaggerated?

	What do the CIs say?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Summary

	Design beats analysis

	Simulate before you collect

	Low power misleads, as well as missing things

	A result depends on what came before it, and on what was filtered out

	Choose your questions and models for scientific reasons

	Report what you see clearly, what you don't, and how you got there
