UNIT 3: Statistical philosophy

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Overview

	We use statistics to confirm effects, estimate parameters, and
	predict outcomes

	I think smoking is bad for lung capacity in children

		\emph{Confirmation:} Children who smoke have lower exhalation
		volumes (ev)

		\emph{Estimation:} The average non-smoker has an ev 1.1--1.3
		times higher than a smoker

		\emph{Prediction:} A 14-year-old male smoker will smoker will
		have an ev of 2.6--3.0 L/s

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Example: Vitamin A

	We compare health indicators of children treated or not treated with
	vitamin A supplements 

		\emph{Estimate:} how much healthier (or less healty) are the
		treated children on average?

		\emph{Confirmation:} are we sure that the supplements are helping
		(or hurting)?

		\emph{Range of estimates:} how much do we think the supplement is
		helping?

----------------------------------------------------------------------

P values and confidence intervals

	We use \emph{P values} to say how sure we are that we have seen a
	positive effect

	We use \emph{confidence intervals} to say what we think is going on
	(with a certain level of confidence)

	P values are \emph{over-rated}

	\emph{Never} use a high P value as (direct) evidence for anything,
	e.g.:
	
		that an effect is small

		that two quantities are similar

	What does a P value actually measure?

P values

	Activity: What is a P value?

		ANS The probability of finding a result at least this “extreme”
		under the null hypothesis

	What does it mean if I find a “significant P value” for some effect
	in this experiment?

		ANS A difference this big would be unlikely under the null
		hypothesis

			ANS So what! I already know vitamin A has strong effects on
			metabolism

	If I'm certain that the true answer isn't exactly zero, why do I
	want the P value anyway?

Frequentist paradigm

	Choose an allowable mistake rate, $\alpha$

		ANS Almost always 0.05

		COMMENT Because Fisher made that up as an example!

	If we do a lot of studies, we don't want to be wrong more than
	$\alpha$ of the time 

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Frequentist P-value logic

	Hypothetically (or counter-factually) if the null is true, what will
	my P value be?

		ANS 5% of the time it will be $<5%$

		ANS $\alpha$ of the time it will be $<\alpha$.

		ANS This meets my frequentist requirement

----------------------------------------------------------------------

Comparing different tails

	Let's say I have four outcomes and my null hypothesis is that they
	are all equally likely.

		If I observe 10% (instead of 25%) is that more or less “extreme”
		than observing 35%?

	I start with 10g of calcium in a mesocosm and expect that to say the
	same?

		What lower value is as extreme as 20g?

		ANS 0g, or 5g, or something in between

----------------------------------------------------------------------

DEFHEAD $\alpha$ spending

	JD: In simple situations it's usually either equivalent or better to
	\emph{not} compare different directions

		Just ask what's the probability of seeing something this extreme
		or more in this direction ---

			ANS But you don't want to double your probability of being
			wrong
 
		ANS You should divide your cutoff by 2 (spend half of your
		$\alpha$ on each direction)

		ANS It is more common (but less beautiful) to multiply the P
		value by 2.

----------------------------------------------------------------------

The most basic frequentist test

	What would have happened if our treatment had no effect at all?

	How does that compare to what we saw?

	What's the simplest way to do this?

		ANS Just relabel the children!
